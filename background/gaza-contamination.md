# Contamination after bombardment

Background for grid story 4 (the Oxfam report on Gaza). The
dossier is stories/07-gaza-oxfam.md.

## What a contamination assessment measures

Modern bombardment leaves more than rubble: fuel and heavy
metals from munitions, asbestos and industrial chemicals from
destroyed buildings, sewage from broken infrastructure, and
agricultural chemicals from stores and farms. An assessment like
Oxfam's looks for signs of that contamination across the
territory and reports coverage: how much of the area shows it.
The report's figure, 99%, is a coverage figure, which is why the
strip's label repeats the attribution and the wording ("signs of
possible contamination") wherever the number travels.

## Why "possible" is load-bearing

The report's own word is possible: signs of contamination are
evidence of presence, not laboratory confirmation at every site.
The page keeps the word in the context record and the
attribution in the headline because dropping either would
upgrade the claim beyond its source. A number that arrives with
its hedge intact is worth more than a stronger number that lost
it on the way.

## Why an NGO report is news

Oxfam is not a wire service; it is an interested party with a
methodology. Its report is news for two reasons the record
states: both BBC brands carried it as a lead item, and its claim
is a checkable document rather than a quote. The desk's rule for
such stories is in PRESS.md: the organisation's phrase stays in
quotation marks, its name stays attached, and the page adds no
finding of its own.

## What corroboration would look like

An environmental agency or a UN body publishing sampling results
from the territory, confirming or bounding the 99% coverage
figure. Until then the number stands as one named body's
finding, printed as exactly that.
