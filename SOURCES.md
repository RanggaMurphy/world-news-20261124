# Sources - Tuesday, 24 November 2026

Every story on the page was carried by at least two distinct outlets
in the day's cross-feed research before it was selected. The numbers
quoted in the "By the Numbers" strip are taken from the stories
themselves, never rounded or combined. `sources.json` carries the
same record in machine-readable form, including a short context
paragraph and the article URLs for each story.

## Feature stories

1. **US to blacklist Asian AI firm Megaspeed after Nvidia scandal**
   - Carried by: Al Jazeera, BBC News, The Guardian.
   - The story: Washington moved to add Megaspeed to its export
     control list. The firm stands accused of funneling advanced AI
     chips to China, and the case widened when a former executive's
     affair with Nvidia chief Jensen Huang became public.
   - Why it leads: it ties export controls, the AI chip race and a
     corporate scandal into the day's most consequential business
     story, and three major outlets carried it independently.

2. **Three killed and dozens missing after fire at Indonesian factory**
   - Carried by: BBC News, DW, The Guardian.
   - The story: a fire tore through a confectionery factory, killing
     at least three people, with many more feared dead and dozens of
     workers still missing as the search went on.
   - Why it is a feature: a mass-casualty industrial disaster with
     the toll still unresolved at press time.

3. **Federal court clears way for army to detonate bomb-making material in Iowa**
   - Carried by: AP News, The Guardian.
   - The story: a federal appeals court cleared the US Army to
     detonate several tons of degraded bomb-making material at an
     Iowa ammunition plant. Hours earlier Donald Trump had halted
     the planned demolition and asked the court to sign off on what
     he called the safest way to resolve the situation first, making
     his go-ahead subject to the incoming governor-elect as well.
   - Why it is a feature: an unusual public reversal followed by a
     court ruling, on a story with real local stakes.

## What we did not print

The fact left off the page for each story, and the reason. A
page without body text omits nearly everything by construction;
these are the omissions a reader would most plausibly ask about.

- **Megaspeed:** how the alleged funneling worked. The page
  prints the accusation; the routing detail stays in the linked
  reporting, because an honest one-image page cannot carry it.
- **Factory fire:** the factory's name and its owner. The wires
  led with the toll and the missing, and the page follows the
  wires.
- **Iowa:** an exact tonnage. "Several tons" is the figure both
  carriers agreed on; a more precise number ran in one outlet's
  copy only, and single-outlet numbers do not reach the page.
- **Merkury:** the drone type and where it launched from. Both
  carriers reported the sinking and the damage; the technical
  detail was not in both, so the page does not carry it.
- **Libya reaction:** Libya's condemnation sits in the context
  record rather than the headline, which belongs to the two
  principals the wire pair named.
- **COP31:** positions country by country. An analysis
  summarises blocs; the page prints the summary and leaves the
  roll call to the linked reporting.
- **Gaza:** how the report reached its figure. The 99% runs
  with its attribution (Oxfam says) precisely because the page
  cannot show the working behind it.

## Headline fidelity

Every headline on the page is a carrying outlet's headline as
published. One liberty only was taken, and it is typographic:
the Oxfam headline's quotation marks are single in the page's
serif, as they are in the outlets' own rendering. Nothing was
rewritten for sharpness, and no number was rounded anywhere, in
a headline or in the strip. The day produced one hard figure,
99%, and the page prints it exactly as reported.

## Selection notes

Seven stories is simply what the two-outlet rule yielded from
the day's feeds; nobody assembled a balanced ticket by hand.
Three consequences deserve a line each:

- Sports and markets are absent because no story in either
  field, in the day's feeds, had two of the tracked outlets
  behind it in a form the format could carry. The rule excluded
  them; no preference did.
- The Libya strike appears twice, once per wire pair. The rule
  counts carriers per story, and each telling passed it on its
  own. A merged version would have read stronger and recorded
  less honestly how the news arrived.
- Gaza is present as an assessment published that day rather
  than an event of the day. Two BBC brands led with it, and its
  99% was the day's most checkable figure, which is also why the
  numbers strip opens with a figure from the news instead of a
  count of the paper itself.

## The confirmation paths

Each story reached the page by a different route, and the routes
are worth recording because they explain the edition's shape:

- **Megaspeed** was confirmed by volume: three outlets, two of
  them with their own Washington coverage, all in the same cycle.
  A story carried that widely does not need a second look; it
  needs a decision about where it leads, and it led.
- **The factory fire** was confirmed by complement: BBC and the
  Guardian reported the event while DW supplied the headline's
  exact shape (killed and missing). No single outlet held the
  whole story; the page's version is the intersection of three.
- **Iowa** was confirmed by statement: the Guardian printed
  Trump's own words in full, and AP's independent report matched
  them. When a story turns on what a principal said, one verbatim
  statement plus one independent match is the strongest pair a
  wire day offers.
- **The Merkury sinking** was confirmed by wire independence:
  CBS and Reuters, no shared ownership, no shared copy. The
  cleanest confirmation of the day, and the reason a fifteen-word
  headline could be printed without a qualifier.
- **The Libya reaction** was confirmed by the same method one
  step removed: AP and BBC carried the principals' words, and the
  principals (Zelensky, the Kremlin, the Libyan government) did
  not contradict each other about what had been said, only about
  what it meant.
- **COP31** was confirmed by repetition inside one owner: the
  analysis ran as a lead item on both BBC brands. Repetition is
  weaker than independence, which is why this story, alone on the
  page, carries its confirmation method as a disclosed caveat.
- **Gaza/Oxfam** was confirmed the same way, with one difference:
  the underlying claim belongs to a named body whose report is a
  document a reader can open. A checkable document behind a
  repeated claim is stronger than repetition alone.

## The production sequence

1. The feeds were scanned and every story with two carriers was
   listed, regardless of subject.
2. The list was cut to seven by the selection rules in PRESS.md.
3. sources.json was written from the record, story by story.
4. Thumbnails were taken from the carrying articles.
5. The page was rendered and audited; the Iowa headline's fit was
   the only one that needed a second look.
6. The documents were written from the same record, then the
   checkers were run: render.py's own audit, check.py from the
   data, verify.py from the documents.
7. The image was committed unchanged from its first render; no
   later step altered a pixel.
